# Microsecond Stereo Sync: Why σ<100µs Is a Hard Gate for Trainable Ego Data

Embodied training data is often misread as "just video." But in binocular ego data, the left and right streams carry geometric meaning only if they are strictly aligned in time — depth, pose, and action labels all rest on that alignment. We place the binocular exposure-sync error σ as the first hard metric of the trainable tier precisely because it sets the physical ceiling on how trainable a clip can be.

Start with why this is not a "looks aligned" problem. Stereo depth rests on Z = f·b/d: the horizontal coordinate difference d of the same world point between the left and right images is the disparity, from which depth is recovered. The formula silently assumes the two frames were captured at the same instant. Real ego data, however, is a moving scene — a hand grasping, a part sliding, a head turning. If the left and right shutters fire an offset apart, a moving object lands at different positions in the two views, and that false disparity is read as real disparity, corrupting depth. The smaller the offset, the more trustworthy the depth.

In magnitude, sync error σ times scene motion speed converts into an equivalent geometric misalignment. Taking ~1 m/s as typical hand/end-effector speed, σ=1 ms yields roughly 1 mm of misalignment, while σ=100 µs pushes it to ~0.1 mm. Browsing, demos, and scene inventory are insensitive to 1 mm — which is why the watchable tier allows σ<1 ms. But for grasp and assembly data destined to train embodied models, millimeter-level geometric error distorts 3D hand pose and depth supervision into systematic label noise. σ<100 µs is the trainable-tier gate; the evaluation tier tightens it further to σ<10 µs.

One easily missed detail: σ is only a standard deviation and hides the shape of the distribution. In one measurement, the vast majority of frames may align well while a few sporadic frames jitter by milliseconds — enough to ruin the depth labels of the clips containing them. That is why the trainable tier asks not for "a σ number" but for a measured histogram of exposure-sync error, using LED/strobe events that trigger both cameras at once, so long-tail bad frames have nowhere to hide.

Reaching σ<100 µs is not a matter of post-hoc software alignment; it is the product of three things working together: timestamping each frame at its exposure midpoint rather than readout-complete; giving the camera, IMU, and timestamp counter a single clock domain; and locking the left/right shutters and IMU sampling to one clock source via hardware FSYNC pins. Software alignment can only make things "look" aligned; hardware phase-locking makes them physically aligned.

In one line: sync error is not an isolated metric — it is the error source at the head of the binocular geometry pipeline. Every order of magnitude you relax σ shrinks the trust radius of downstream depth by the same order. Treating σ<100 µs as a hard gate is, at bottom, giving "trainable" a physical definition — the hardest line between the material tier and the trainable tier in the EGO-S2 four-tier framework.
